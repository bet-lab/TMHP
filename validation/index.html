Validation¶

A physics-based model is only useful if its first-principles answers land close to the real machines it is meant to describe. This page is that check.

The harder question is whether it keeps landing close when the machine changes. A model tuned to one unit will match that unit; what tells you something is whether the same defaults, unchanged, follow a different refrigerant, a different size, and a different equipment architecture.

How to read these numbers

Nothing here is fitted to anything here. Every unit is run with the library defaults exactly as shipped. The only per-unit inputs are values the manufacturer publishes about the machine itself — nameplate capacity, refrigerant, and, where the manufacturer publishes them at all, compressor displacement and rated air flow. Those have the same status as the nameplate: specification, not tuning. Where the evidence for a default came from is a separate page, Where the defaults come from.

Points the model could not evaluate are reported, not dropped. The counts below distinguish points attempted from points evaluated, and the interactive table shows each unevaluated point with its reason. A point at which the model runs but delivers more than 1 % less than the published duty (a capacity ceiling) is not scored either, with the reason capacity_short: its COP belongs to a different operating point. The same holds at the other end: a published duty below the smallest one the model runs continuously is reported as the mean of on/off cycling (capacity_clamped == "min"), which is not what a catalogue row is, and is left out with the reason cycling_mean. No point of the present catalogues is in that case.

Reading the residuals¶

There is no single headline number: the two families answer different questions, and the set mixes rating standards (EN 14511 and AHRI 210/240) that define COP differently, so each family is reported on its own and nothing is pooled across standards. The two held catalogues are run and written but never enter a headline. The compressor efficiencies are the shipped ossorio-r290-te-2026-11-21 maps (Where the defaults come from), the same for every unit, applied unchanged, inside the compressor operating envelope (Reference points and operating limits). Three properties of those maps are read with every figure below:

  • They are one compressor’s. The maps are fitted to 134 measured points of one 46 cm³/rev R290 scroll. Every unit runs on them, whatever its refrigerant and its compressor: the rotary and swing compressors of the set as much as its scrolls. A particular machine is followed by calibrating the maps (#90), which no figure on this page does.

  • Outside their data they are followed as they are. The data cover 30 to 110 rev/s and a hull in pressure ratio and evaporating temperature; outside it the result carries efficiency_extrapolated.

  • The electrical input is taken at the inverter input. The maps are fitted on the power of compressor and inverter together; there is no separate drive model.

The previous release (the semi-empirical models of 2026-11-15, committed results of before #217) gave the air-to-air set 8.5 % MAPE and −5.1 % bias, and the air-to-water set 8.3 % and −5.0 % (136 of 153 points evaluated).

8 air-to-water points are not evaluated, all of them because the model delivers more than 1 % less than the published duty (capacity_short); their COP belongs to a smaller operating point and is not compared with the catalogue’s full-load COP. Three run at the 120 rev/s speed ceiling and deliver 93 to 96 % of the duty: WH-WXG16 at −15 °C outdoor, at 45, 55 and 65 °C leaving water. All three meet the published duty with the frost and defrost adjustment switched off (Frost and defrost): the mean over frosting and defrost needs a larger frost-free duty than the ceiling allows. The other five are the Samsung unit at 65 °C leaving water, held at 72 to 88 % of the duty by the 65 °C condensing limit of R32 (capacity_binding == "condensing_temperature"). No point stops on the discharge-temperature limit; other air-to-water points are evaluated under the discharge-temperature control, with wet suction or reduced superheat where it acts.

Every air-to-air point is evaluated, the held Fujitsu grids included.

No validation machine has a scroll without injection, the compressor the maps describe: the four scrolls of the set have vapour or flash injection, which the single-stage cycle does not represent, and the six others are rotaries. The held Fujitsu units publish a rotary compressor; their bias went from +33.6 % in the previous release to +13.4 % (all 244). The Daikin units publish a swing compressor.

The biases are stated here, not corrected. Air-to-air cooling runs low at every outdoor temperature, from −13.6 % at 20 °C to −12.3 % at 40 °C outdoor. The dry indoor coil (see Cooling and latent load) does not make it optimistic: the cooling rows with the least latent load (sensible heat ratio 0.95 or above, 25 rows) run −17.1 %, and those with the most latent load (below 0.75, 79 rows) −8.8 %. It is part of what holds cooling low: run with the room humidity of each row, the wet coil is at −3.1 % over the same rows, +7.0 % on the wettest and −15.5 % on the driest (② below). At the rating point the three smallest Daikin units, with the highest nameplate EER, sit 12 to 16 % low (①). Air-to-air heating runs low at every outdoor temperature but the coldest, the frost-free ones included: with the frost and defrost adjustment (the EnergyPlus DX heating-coil model, its constants unchanged; Air-source heat pump (ASHP — space conditioning)) the bias is −19.5 % at 0 °C, −17.0 % at −5 °C, −11.6 % at −10 °C and +1.4 % at −15 °C outdoor, against −10.4 % and −11.6 % at 6 and 10 °C, where the coil does not frost. Without the adjustment the same rows run +5.3 %, +2.5 %, +4.5 % and +15.8 %. Air-to-water carries the same adjustment and runs lowest where it applies (Frost and defrost): the bias is −3.8 % over the 63 points at +7 °C outdoor and above, where the coil does not frost, −12.0 % at +2 °C, −5.6 % at −7 °C and −4.3 % below −7 °C. Without the adjustment the same rows run +13.7 % at +2 °C, +12.6 % at −7 °C and +9.6 % below. Split by injection, the machines without it run −2.4 % frost-free, −11.7 % at +2 °C, −3.6 % at −7 °C and −2.3 % below, the injection machines −6.2 %, −12.5 %, −9.4 % and −8.6 %. The adjustment follows the load on the coil (frost at part load), but these catalogues do not test that: every air-to-water point at or below +2 °C, and all but four of the air-to-air ones (at −15 °C, +0.2 to +1.2 % in COP), is at or above the capacity the adjustment’s correlation is written for, so the figures on this page are those of the full correction. What the load term changes is the part-load trajectory of Part-load behaviour and Where the defaults come from.

The Fujitsu offset, large and of one sign in heating, was checked before being published rather than after.

The offset is three things, not one

An earlier version of this page attributed the whole gap to hardware efficiency. That was one of three causes presented as the explanation, and this is the correction. Comparing each machine at its own nominal rating point – no forced maximum output, the cleanest comparison available – separates them. The cooling rating rows are not free of latent load (sensible heat ratio 0.66 for the Fujitsu units, 0.69 to 1.00 for the five Daikin units); that is the second cause below.

① Residual hardware efficiency (about 20 points at the heating rating point). At the nominal heating rating point Daikin lands between −11 and −6 % and Fujitsu at +11 to +12 %; at the cooling rating point Daikin spans −16 to −1 %, falling with nameplate EER, and Fujitsu +14 to +21 %. That part is real and it is the defaults’ responsibility: the two product lines are built to different efficiency targets and a rule written per kilowatt cannot tell which one it is looking at.

② Dehumidification, cooling half. The results on this page are those of the dry indoor coil, which carries the whole published capacity as sensible heat. At its rating point the Fujitsu removes 34 % of its duty as latent heat (SHR 0.66) against 2 % for the Daikin RXM25A (SHR 0.98; the five Daikin units go from 1.00 for the 2.0 kW unit to 0.69 for the 5.0 kW one). Within the Daikin set alone, the bias of the 180 cooling points correlates with their sensible heat ratio at r = −0.73: the more latent load, the higher the model’s COP against the catalogue’s. The dry coil is not what raises it. To move the same total heat it has to run colder than a coil that also condenses water, which costs lift and COP; run with the wet coil, the Daikin rows are higher in every band, the wettest most, and the correlation is stronger (Cooling and latent load). The held Fujitsu rows, run the same way at the indoor wet bulb their tables print, go the same direction: from +0.4 % to +4.0 % and from +8.5 % to +14.4 %.

③ Maximum-capacity tables, heating half. Fujitsu’s heating grid is published at maximum capacity, with the compressor pinned at full speed where its efficiency is worst. The harness asks for that same duty and the model meets it well below its speed ceiling (median 71 and 74 rev/s of 120). Different operating point; the comparison flatters the model.

Only ① is a property of the defaults. ② is a stated model boundary and ③ is a property of the source document.

Note

The band must be quoted on the same basis as the rule. Component catalogues report conductance per unit of coil duty; the rule is written against the nameplate, and the two differ by 1 + 1/EER = 1.307. An earlier version of this page carried the median across but not the band, which made the default look better placed in its own population than it is.

Why the default was not moved

Moving the conductance away from Q/5 would have improved the numbers on this page for the machines at one end of the band at the cost of the other. It was not done. The value was derived from the catalogue population before any parity result existed, and changing it afterwards to improve a parity plot is calibration wearing validation’s clothes — the exact thing this harness is built to prevent. The spread is reported instead. The approach-temperature defaults that replaced Q/5 in November 2026 were fixed the same way, from an archive of machine data and a hold-out rule, before the parity results were read.

Three smaller patterns, also named rather than left for a reader to find:

The air-to-water set rests on published displacements; the air-to-air set does not. Those units publish compressor displacement, which no air-to-air unit in the set does — neither Daikin nor Fujitsu prints one, and a search of five data books, three service manuals and two specification sheets found no displacement for any of them. So the air-to-air panel is also a test of the library’s default displacement rather than of a specification input.

Both air-to-air refrigerants are present for a reason. Daikin’s pair-application range is entirely R-32. Without the Fujitsu R-410A units that panel would say nothing about whether the physics carries across working fluids. It is worth being explicit that the offset above is not a refrigerant effect: the three R-410A air-to-water units sit at 5.3 to 8.9 % MAPE, inside the 5.2 to 11.9 % spread of the air-to-water set.

The one air-to-water outlier turns out to be the same story. The 16 kW R32 unit runs +5.1 % optimistic where the set runs −5.9 % (its 9 and 12 kW siblings −10.9 and −4.7 %). Neither of its published inputs explains it: substituting its 12 kW sibling’s rated air flow moves the bias by 2.2 points, and replacing its published displacement with the library default by 1.5 points (all 15 points evaluated in each run). What explains it is the catalogue itself. At every one of the fifteen conditions, the 16 kW machine’s published COP is 8 to 22 % below the 9 kW machine’s from the same product line:

Efficiency is not constant per kilowatt as you go up a product line, and a rule written per kilowatt cannot know that. The model treats the three units as near-identical machines at different sizes, which is what the rules say; the manufacturer’s own data says the largest is about 16 % worse. The residual is that gap.

This is the Fujitsu finding again at smaller scale and inside a single manufacturer’s range, which is worth noticing: the limitation is not one unusual product line, it is capacity-normalised scaling itself. The defaults describe a typical machine of a given size; they do not know that a manufacturer’s 16 kW model is built to a different cost target than its 9 kW model.

The Samsung high-temperature unit is outside the model boundary. Its catalogue names a scroll compressor with flash injection, which the single-stage cycle does not model, and it publishes no displacement. Its five points at 65 °C leaving water are not evaluated: the 65 °C condensing limit of R32 holds them at 72 to 88 % of the published duty. It is reported in the injection stratum above, stated rather than corrected for.

Capacity¶

The capacity ratio is the duty the model delivers at the catalogue request divided by the published duty. The capacity score includes short points. A point that falls more than 1 % short contributes to the capacity score and is excluded from the COP score. Capacity scores are grouped by catalogue status and model class. The held group is diagnostic. In summary.csv, the class_ columns describe the model class and status of the catalogue on that row. In manifest.json, capacity_by_status_and_model_class uses the status keys adopted and hold for the groups present in the results.

Compressor speed binds three of the air-to-water points, and the condensing temperature limit binds five. Probe headroom is the probed duty divided by the published duty. For air-to-air, the probe uses max_capacity(). For air-to-water, it requests 1.5 times the published duty at the same outdoor temperature, entering water temperature and water flow. The multiplier allows the probe to reach limits above the published duty with a bounded overload request. Meeting the overload within the relative validation tolerance of 10⁻⁶ establishes a lower bound at the requested multiplier (150 % headroom). A smaller return beyond that tolerance gives a ceiling. For either model, if the probe returns a lower duty than the ordinary solve within the same tolerance, the probe records the duty from the ordinary solve. A larger drop or a failed probe leaves the estimate missing.

Among scored air-to-water points, COP MAPE and bias were 7.9 % and −6.7 % without discharge control (124 points), 8.3 % and −5.9 % with reduced superheat (5 points), and 8.2 % and +0.4 % with wet suction (16 points). All 360 scored air-to-air points ran without discharge control (12.6 % MAPE and −12.1 % bias). Scored adopted points with a missing control flag, or with control active and missing suction quality, are counted in the unknown stratum.

Cooling and latent load¶

The parity results on this page are those of the dry indoor coil: the model carries the whole published cooling capacity as sensible heat. Manufacturer cooling grids publish total capacity, part of which is dehumidification, and each cooling point in the catalogue files records its sensible heat ratio for this reason, and its indoor wet bulb with it. The catalogues print the sensible and the total capacity; the ratio in the files is their quotient, rounded to two decimals. Heating points have no latent component.

AirSourceHeatPump can also be given the room humidity, and its indoor coil then condenses water below the room’s dew point (Wet indoor coil (cooling)). A one-off study runs the 180 Daikin cooling points both ways — dry, and wet at the indoor dry bulb and wet bulb each row is published at (validation/wet_coil_shr/simulate.py, results in validation/results/wet_coil_shr_once/). Its dry pass is the shipped one, row for row. The model’s sensible heat ratio is taken net of the indoor fan’s heat, as the published capacities are. The two held Fujitsu units are run the same way and reported apart, at the end of this section.

Sensible heat ratio. The wet coil gives 0.81 against the published 0.79 on average, with a mean absolute difference of 0.047 and a correlation of r = 0.95 over the 180 rows; single rows differ by −0.15 to +0.10. It moves less with the size of the unit than the catalogue does: 0.04 low for the 2.0 kW unit (0.90 against 0.94), 0.08 high for the 5.0 kW unit (0.74 against 0.66). Put as a temperature: over the 172 rows that publish a ratio below 1, the model’s evaporating temperature is on average 1.0 K above the coil temperature at which its own coil line gives the published ratio (−2.0 to +3.7 K by row; 0.4 K below it for the 2.0 kW unit, 3.0 K above for the 5.0 kW unit). The band at 0.95 and above is not a dry band: 17 of its 25 rows publish a ratio below 1, and the model’s coil condenses water on 24 of them.

COP. Over these rows the wet coil runs its evaporator 3.1 K warmer than the dry one for the same total heat and draws 9.4 % less — less on 179 of the 180 rows, which is what these conditions give and not a property of a wet coil (Wet indoor coil (cooling)). The bias goes from −12.8 % to −3.1 % and the mean absolute error from 12.8 % to 9.6 %. The gain is not even. It grows with the latent load — 15.8 points where the published ratio is below 0.75, 1.5 points at 0.95 and above — so the wet coil passes the catalogue at one end and stays low at the other: +7.0 % where the published ratio is below 0.75, −15.5 % at 0.95 and above, and the correlation of the bias with the ratio goes from r = −0.73 to −0.92. By unit the dry coil runs from −16.8 % (2.0 kW) to −5.0 % (5.0 kW) and the wet coil from −13.4 % to +12.6 %: closer to the catalogue for four units, further from it for the largest. The dry coil therefore holds the cooling results of this page low, by 9.7 points on average, and it is not the whole of their low bias: the rows with the least latent load run 15 to 17 % low with either coil. The study does not say where that comes from, nor why the wettest rows pass the catalogue: the indoor conductance rule (0.16 W/K per W of nameplate, provisional, not derived for a wet coil), the compressor level and the airflow all enter. The size trend at least is the rule’s: a conductance solved from each row’s published ratio is nearly constant within a unit (1.8–3.3 % scatter over its rows) and runs from 382 W/K (2.0 kW) to 337 W/K (5.0 kW), where the rule gives 320 to 800 W/K; with it the spread of the unit biases goes from 26.1 to 15.3 points (#220, #237). As a sensitivity, not a calibration: with the indoor coil’s rated conductance halved the wet-coil bias is −9.4 % and the ratio 0.71 (0.08 low); doubled, −1.0 % and 0.86 (0.07 high). A smaller coil runs colder, which lowers the ratio and the COP together: the three values show that trade-off, and none of them is a best one.

The capacity the comparison runs at. The harness passes the published capacity as the request, which the model takes as the heat of the coil, before the indoor fan’s heat; the published capacity is the net one. Over the wet rows the room therefore receives 20.4 W (0.62 %) less than published on average, and more than 1 % less on 1 row. With the request raised until the net delivery is the published capacity (20.4 W more on average) the COP is 0.19 % lower: the bias is −13.2 % dry and −3.3 % wet, on all 180 rows, and nothing above turns on the difference. The coil temperature a published ratio implies is computed with that ratio taken as the coil’s own; putting the ratio on the coil’s boundary with the model’s fan heat raises it by 0.04 K on average.

The held Fujitsu units. The manual prints the indoor condition of its cooling tables as six dry bulb / wet bulb pairs, so the 144 cooling rows of the two units are run wet as well. They are held catalogues (AHRI 210/240; the manual states neither what its input power includes nor whether its capacities are net of the indoor fan’s heat), and nothing of theirs enters a figure above.

Scope¶

What has and has not been benchmarked

AirSourceHeatPumpBoiler and AirSourceHeatPump are benchmarked against manufacturer catalogues here. The ground-source and water-source families (GroundSourceHeatPumpBoiler, WaterSourceHeatPumpBoiler, GroundSourceHeatPump, and the subsystem-augmented variants) share the same refrigerant-cycle core. The test suite only checks that the three base models solve a representative operating point (tests/test_models_smoke.py: one tank-heating point for each boiler, a heating and a cooling point for the ground-source unit; each must converge and deliver the requested duty). That shows they run, not that their numbers are right: none has been compared against unit-specific data. Their liquid-coupled faces take the approach temperatures of the coaxial and plate exchangers of real machines (Where the defaults come from), not the air-coil values.

The default compressor displacement of every family, the ground- and water-source boilers included, is the one a compressor catalogue gives for the capacity and the refrigerant (tmhp.default_inputs.estimate_displacement(), Where the defaults come from). For the liquid-source families no unit data exist to judge it.

Reproduce¶

uv sync --locked --all-groups
scripts/regen-validation.sh                         # every catalogue, the site data and these figures
uv run python -m validation.parity.run --unit <slug>   # one catalogue

Each unit run records what produced it in validation/results/provenance/<slug>.json — the commit, any uncommitted edits, the src/tmhp and harness tree hashes, the coefficient version, the Python, CoolProp and platform. The summary refuses units whose records disagree, and validation/results/manifest.json carries what they share. tests/test_validation_results_current.py reruns every committed point and fails when the model no longer reproduces it.

Adding a machine is a transcription and nothing else — see Adding a catalogue.
