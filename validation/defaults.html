Where the defaults come from¶

TMHP decides a lot on your behalf. Hand it a capacity and a refrigerant and it produces heat-exchanger conductances, a compressor displacement, a rated air flow and the efficiency model of a compressor. That convenience is the point of the library — but it is only worth having if each of those numbers can be traced to a published document rather than to somebody’s judgement, and if you can rerun the step that produced it.

This page is that trace. Every row links to the source and to the script that reads it.

The one rule behind all of it

Published literature reports the conductance, displacement or efficiency of particular machines. Nobody publishes a rule expressed per unit of capacity — which is exactly what a model that sizes its own components needs. So the numbers below are not taken from a paper. They are derived from populations: manufacturer catalogues that rate an entire product range at one declared condition, which makes models of different capacity comparable by construction. The citations are for the method, the rating standard and the correlation; the numbers are measured across many machines.

A consequence worth stating plainly: validating against a catalogue is never calibrating against it. No parameter below is fitted to any unit in the validation set. One choice between two candidate compressor speed shapes was made on certified heat-pump data, and it is stated where it was made (Compressor data, not heat-pump data).

Heat-exchanger conductance¶

The problem¶

Every model generates its heat-exchanger conductances from a single declared capacity, so what has to be established is a capacity-normalised rule for each exchanger — not any particular machine’s conductance. Real machines do not publish a conductance either. What their service manuals, installation manuals and test reports print at a rated point is the refrigerant pressure at a service or compressor port, next to the entering air or water temperature, the flow and the capacity. That is evidence at the level of the heat exchanger, and it is what the cycle needs: the conductance is only the means by which the model arrives at the saturation temperature the compressor works against.

What the conductance means¶

Every refrigerant-side exchanger in TMHP is an isothermal refrigerant against a single-phase fluid, ε = 1 − exp(−UA/C) and Q = ε C |T_in − T_sat|. There is no refrigerant pressure drop and no desuperheating or subcooling zone, so the model’s saturation temperature has to be the one at the compressor’s ports for the pressure ratio to be right. A default conductance is therefore defined as the effective conductance that reproduces, at the rated point, the saturation temperature at the pressure the machine prints. Line and coil pressure drop and the single-phase zones are inside it; it is not the U·A one would compute from the coil geometry.

Which approach temperature transfers¶

For one operating point, the inlet temperature difference ITD = |T_in − T_sat|, the leaving-end approach ITD − Q/C, the LMTD and UA/Q convert into each other exactly. The choice only matters when a value measured on one machine is carried to another of a different capacity and flow: what is held fixed? That is a question the data can answer. Per exchanger and refrigerant role, each candidate law is fitted with one product line held out (product lines that share hardware count as one) and scored by how well it predicts the saturation temperature of the held-out units at their own flow and duty. The rule, fixed before the data were collected: the simplest law within 10 % of the lowest error ships, if the stratum has at least three product lines and two manufacturers.

The leaving-end approach wins or ties in every stratum a model uses, and a fixed inlet difference — the obvious convention — is the worst everywhere. The reason shows in the data: manufacturers build several capacities on one chassis, and on a shared coil the inlet difference grows with the load while the leaving approach stays put. Holding it fixed gives the conductance in closed form,

\[UA = C \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{\dot Q}{C\,\Delta T_{\mathrm{out}}}\right)\]

with C the fluid’s heat capacity rate at the reference flow and Q̇ the exchanger’s own duty. A solution exists for every positive duty and flow. A fixed inlet difference has none once Q̇ ≥ C · ITD — a reference flow 15 % below the one assumed can be enough — and conductance per unit capacity ignores the flow altogether.

The duty is the exchanger’s, not the nameplate: as a condenser the outdoor coil of a split unit rejects 1.24 W per watt of cooling nameplate (the nameplate plus the compressor work), and as an evaporator absorbs 0.76 W per watt of heating nameplate, both archive medians (tmhp.default_inputs.hx_duty_per_nameplate()). The reference flow is the rated airflow of the coil (Maximum fan airflow), the rated loop flow, or the rated circulation flow of a plate condenser.

These values were not chosen by catalogue COP: the laws and their constants come from the archive and the hold-out rule alone, and the catalogue comparison of Validation was run only after they were fixed. With them the comparison still under-predicts, so the conductance is not where the remaining bias sits.

Every default conductance is recorded in model.default_input_estimates["UA_…"] with its law, the spread of the population, the hold-out error, the sources and whether the duty lies outside the archive’s range (extrapolated). A conductance you pass is used as given and has no entry there.

How wide the band is, and what that costs you

The leaving approach of a stratum is a median. Between the 10th and 90th percentile of the product lines it spans 0.9–3.9 K for outdoor coils as condensers, 2.0–4.5 K as evaporators, 0.7–3.3 K for plate condensers, 1.0–6.6 K and 1.6–6.5 K for coaxial source exchangers as evaporators and condensers, and 1.5–6.6 K for water-to-air indoor coils. That width is real hardware, not measurement noise: at the same duty and flow, two machines at either end of the outdoor condensing band condense about 3 K apart. Even the best law misses the saturation temperature of a held-out product line by 1.2 to 2.9 K (RMSE).

So a single vendor-neutral default describes a typical machine, and will under-predict a high-efficiency product line and over-predict a lower-efficiency one. If you know the machine, pass its conductances (UA_ou_rated, UA_ou_evap_rated, UA_iu_rated, UA_tank_hx …). Validation backs the conductance out of each validation unit’s own rating point, and decomposes the rest of the residual, which is not all conductance.

Note

Quote the band on the same basis as the rule. The archive’s spread is a spread of leaving approach; its spread in UA per watt also carries the spread of airflow per watt, and is wider.

Where this does not apply

  • Wall-mounted indoor coils. No value is shipped. Every row that gives duty, airflow and pressure at one point comes from one manufacturer (Mitsubishi Electric, six product lines), and the evidence disagrees on the quantity the law uses: 6.8 K of leaving approach on the declared duty and airflow, about 3.6 K on the discharge-air temperature the same tables print, about 3.3 K from a second manufacturer’s pipe temperatures at an assumed rated load. The inlet difference agrees at 22 K. So AirSourceHeatPump keeps UA_iu_rated = 0.16 × hp_capacity, no longer tied to the outdoor coil, and records it as provisional with that reason.

  • Frosted operation. Frosting rows are excluded; frost and defrost are handled by the defrost adjustment, not by the conductance.

  • Microchannel coils. The archive has none; the strata are fin-and-tube coils.

  • Wet cooling coils. Indoor coils run wet in cooling and TMHP’s coils are dry, so those rows are excluded. The indoor values come from the dry condensing role and are used in both modes.

  • Immersed or wrapped tank coils. No measured row pairs a condensing pressure with a tank temperature, so no value is issued. Build the boiler with m_dot_w_rated=0 and pass UA_tank_hx.

  • Water-to-water machines. The archive’s water-to-water rows come from one manufacturer whose heating tables print suction pressures below their own low-pressure cut-out; the liquid-source boilers borrow the water-to-air coaxial evaporator.

The boilers’ sink¶

The tank heat exchanger of AirSourceHeatPumpBoiler, GroundSourceHeatPumpBoiler and WaterSourceHeatPumpBoiler is an external plate condenser fed with tank water by a circulation pump, the structure of monobloc air-to-water units. T_tank_w is the water entering the condenser. The rated circulation flow is m_dot_w_rated = hp_capacity / (c_w · dT_w_rated) with dT_w_rated 5 K (the EN 14511 30/35 °C rating), and the heat the exchanger carries per kelvin of condensing temperature above the entering water is K_tank_hx = C_w (1 − exp(−UA_tank_hx / C_w)). AirSourceHeatPumpBoiler takes a per-call m_dot_w; the liquid-source boilers run at m_dot_w_rated. m_dot_w_rated=0 selects the earlier form without circulating water, UA_tank_hx working against the tank temperature directly; with UA_tank_hx = hp_capacity / 5 it is the earlier machine.

A rating condition names the leaving water (W35, W55). The catalogue comparisons and operating_range() hand a boiler the water EN 14511 enters with — the leaving water less the standard’s rise, 5 K up to 45 °C, 8 K at 55 °C, 10 K at 65 °C — and, for AirSourceHeatPumpBoiler, the flow that carries the duty at that rise (tmhp.standard_conditions.en14511_water_rise(), tmhp.standard_conditions.en14511_condenser_water()). This replaces the earlier convention of a tank 2.5 K below the leaving water with no flow.

The previous rule¶

Until November 2026 the outdoor coil of AirSourceHeatPump had UA_ou = hp_capacity / 5 in both roles and its indoor coil 0.8 × UA_ou; the boilers had UA_tank_hx = hp_capacity / 5 without circulating water, the air-source boiler’s outdoor coil 0.7 × UA_tank_hx, and the ground-source models 0.18 × hp_capacity on the source side and 0.08 × hp_capacity for the indoor coil. hp_capacity / 5 came from 1,414 component coils in catalogues rated under EN 328 (352 evaporators, median UA/Q 0.190) and ENV 327 (1,062 condensers, 0.146), converted to the nameplate basis with 1 + 1/EER and corroborated by twelve Trane packaged units’ published coil geometry through the Wang, Chi & Chang (2000) air-side correlation. The scripts under validation/extraction/ (ua_transfer_law, en328_inversion, trane_geometry, dt_convention) still reproduce it.

It was replaced for two reasons. The rating standards fix the temperature difference at which a component coil is rated, so the populations say how much conductance a coil has at that difference, not which approach a heat pump runs at; and residential mini-splits, the product class of the air-to-air validation units, were outside that evidence. For outdoor coils the new evidence lands on almost the same value. An earlier version of that work also reported a pooled regression of UA/Q against air flow (slope −0.020, R² 0.001) as evidence that the two are unrelated; inside one rating standard they are linked by an identity, so the regression meant nothing and was withdrawn, leaving the overlap of the two standards’ bands and the geometric route as the evidence.

Compressor displacement¶

The machine has to move enough refrigerant to carry the nameplate duty at the nameplate condition:

\[V_{\mathrm{disp}} = \frac{\dot Q_{\mathrm{nom}}} {\Delta h \; \rho_{1} \; \eta_{\mathrm{vol}} \; n_{\mathrm{rated}}}\]

Both \(\Delta h\) and \(\rho_1\) come from CoolProp at a declared rating point, so the dependence on the working fluid is pure fluid property rather than a tabulated coefficient. That dependence is not small: per kilowatt of nameplate the displacement runs from about 3.0 cm³/rev for R32 to 7.3 for R134a.

What is left, \(\eta_{\mathrm{vol}}\, n\), belongs to the compressor, and compressor catalogues print it. Each row gives a displacement and a capacity at a declared rating point, so \(\eta_{\mathrm{vol}}\, n = \dot Q / (V \rho_1 \Delta h)\) row by row. Across 41 rows of inverter twin-rotary compressors from two manufacturers (LG and Mitsubishi Electric SCI; 14 families, R32 and R410A, 2.9 to 14.9 kW) it is 53.2 rev/s — 0.887 of the 60 rev/s the rows are published at. Predicting a family, a manufacturer or a refrigerant from the others misses the printed displacement by 2.7, 2.6 and 3.8 %.

The default displacement is that relation at the catalogues’ own rating point (evaporating 7.2 °C, condensing 54.4 °C, suction 18.3 °C, liquid 46.1 °C), with the nameplate capacity standing in for the compressor’s catalogue capacity (tmhp.default_inputs.estimate_displacement()). Every model — AirSourceHeatPump, GroundSourceHeatPump, AirSourceHeatPumpBoiler, GroundSourceHeatPumpBoiler and WaterSourceHeatPumpBoiler — uses it when V_cmp_ref is left out, and says so: model.default_input_estimates["V_cmp_ref"] holds the value, the data release, the capacity range of the catalogue rows, whether the capacity lies outside that range (extrapolated) and the source documents. A V_cmp_ref you pass is used as given and has no entry there.

A ground-source boiler is still rated high — at 8 kW, 74.8 rev/s with R32, 82.1 with R410A, 83.8 with R290 and 96.8 with R134a — because its nameplate condition, B0/W55, is harsher than the catalogue rating point (7.2 / 54.4 °C) at which the rule reads the nameplate: a compressor sized to deliver the nameplate there has to turn faster to deliver it from brine at 0 °C. A machine rated above 80 rev/s takes its speed ceiling from 1.5 times the rated speed (125.6 rev/s for the R290 machine, 145.2 for the R134a one), which is above the 120 rev/s the efficiency data reach. If you know the compressor of such a machine, pass V_cmp_ref and rps_max.

Where the catalogue airflow meets the dry-coil model

The indoor laws give 150 to 160 m³/h per kW for wall-mounted units of 9 kW and above and for floor-standing units of any size. TMHP’s coils are dry, so the whole nameplate is sensible heat, and at that airflow the room air has to change by about 20 K: the refrigerant then stands 20 to 25 K from the room air at nameplate capacity (a 15 kW unit condenses at 42 °C in a 20 °C room and evaporates at 4.6 °C in a 27 °C one).

That is why the approach-temperature search box of AirSourceHeatPump (dT_approach_bounds) is 1 to 30 K — the box the catalogue parity runs have always used. With the earlier upper bound of 20 K the box itself was the limit at the rating point: a default unit reached its nameplate at 35/27 °C only up to 8 kW (98 % at 9 kW, 89 % at 15 kW; a floor-standing unit 96 % at any capacity), and in heating at −7 °C outdoors fell short from 6 kW (86 % at 8 kW). Compressor efficiency¶

The default compressor is the model of a compressor type. The constructor argument compressor_type selects it — rotary_twin_inverter (the default: the inverter twin rotary of residential heat pumps), scroll (a scroll without injection) or rotary_single_fixed (a fixed-speed single rotary, kept as a reference) — and each type is a semi-empirical form fitted to standalone-compressor data, version semi-empirical-2026-11-15 (tmhp.compressor_efficiency). This section says what the forms are, what they were fitted to, how they were chosen and where they are weak, so that you can decide whether to trust the default for your case. The authority for every statement is the archive, validation/coefficients/semi-empirical-2026-11-15/, and its README.

Two things to know before the detail

  • The two rotary types, the default among them, are provisional: their shape rests on five machines, and one decision about it was taken on heat-pump data (Compressor data, not heat-pump data).

  • The efficiency level of all three types is the level manufacturers publish. The measured machines in the archive sit lower, by up to 16 % (Limits and provisional items).

What is modelled, and where each loss sits¶

The fitted quantity is the overall isentropic efficiency on the terminal basis — the electrical power at the compressor terminals, the inverter excluded:

\[\eta_{oi} = \frac{\dot m \,(h_{2s} - h_1)}{P_{\mathrm{terminal}}}\]

A source whose power was measured at the inverter input is divided by the drive model below first, so every source enters on this basis.

A cycle needs more than that one number, because it uses the lost work in two places: the part that reaches the gas raises the discharge enthalpy, and so the heating duty and the discharge temperature; all of it is paid for electrically. The models send a fixed share \(f\) of the loss work \(P - \dot m\, w_s\) to the gas and the rest out of the shell as heat:

\[\eta_{is} = \frac{1}{1 + f\,(1/\eta_{oi} - 1)}, \qquad \eta_{em} = \eta_{oi} + f\,(1 - \eta_{oi})\]

so \(\eta_{is}\,\eta_{em} = \eta_{oi}\), and both lie in (0, 1] whenever \(\eta_{oi}\) does. \(f\) is fitted to measured discharge temperatures: 0.769 for the scroll (818 rows of four machines) and 0.576 for the rotary types (four rows of one machine).

The drive is a separate model, fitted to five measured inverters:

\[\eta_{inv} = 1 - \lambda\,\bigl(1 + \beta\,(60/N - 1)\bigr)\]

with \(N\) the shaft speed in rev/s, \(\beta\) = 0.618 and \(\lambda\) = 0.0588, the median of the five (their quartiles are 0.045 and 0.062). It is 0.941 at 60 rev/s, 0.869 at 20 rev/s and 0.832 at the default speed floor of 15 rev/s.

A model reports three quantities, as it always has: eta_cmp_isen is \(\eta_{is}\), eta_cmp_vol the volumetric efficiency, and eta_cmp is \(\eta_{em}\,\eta_{inv}\). The electrical input of the compressor, E_cmp, is therefore the power at the inverter input. The map shipped until November 2026 had no drive loss; the library applies the drive model to all three types, the fixed-speed one included. All three quantities depend on the suction state — pressure, density and the isentropic enthalpy rise — as well as on the pressure ratio and the speed. An efficiency you pass yourself (eta_cmp_isen=…) is a number or a function of the pressure ratio and the speed and is used as given, drive loss included or not as you decide.

The forms¶

\[ \begin{align}\begin{aligned}\text{scroll:}\quad \frac{1}{\eta_{oi}} = a\,\varphi_z(PR;\, r_v, k, z_v) + q_2\, n\,\frac{10^5}{MEP_s} + c_L\,\frac{PR - 1}{n}\\\text{rotary:}\quad \frac{1}{\eta_{oi}} = 1 + a \left( n\,\frac{10^5}{MEP_s} + c_L\,\frac{PR - 1}{n} + c_u\,\frac{MEP_s / 10^6}{n} \right)\end{aligned}\end{align} \]

\(MEP_s = \rho_1 (h_{2s} - h_1)\) is the isentropic work per unit of swept volume [Pa], \(k\) the effective exponent of the isentropic compression at the suction state, and \(n\) the speed coordinate. Every term is a loss per unit of isentropic power:

  • \(\varphi\) is the work of a machine with a built-in volume ratio \(r_v\) — isentropic compression to \(r_v\), then isochoric work to the discharge pressure — over the isentropic work. A scroll has one, so its efficiency peaks where the pressure ratio matches it and falls on both sides. Below \(PR = r_v^k\) the machine over-compresses, and a share \(z_v\) of that loss is relieved: \(\varphi_z = \varphi - z_v(\varphi - 1)\) there.

  • A rolling-piston rotary has no built-in volume ratio (its discharge valve opens at the discharge pressure), so its base is one and its losses scale together with the level \(a\).

  • \(n \cdot 10^5 / MEP_s\) is a loss power that grows with the square of the speed (viscous friction, flow losses), \(c_L (PR - 1)/n\) leakage that is compressed again, and \(c_u (MEP_s/10^6)/n\) the copper loss of the motor, which goes as the torque squared.

With a level of at least one for the scroll, a nonnegative one for the rotary types and nonnegative coefficients, \(0 < \eta_{oi} \le 1\) is a property of these forms.

The exponential is the re-expansion of the clearance gas, written so that it stays positive; the denominator is the share that is delivered when the gas in the chamber leaves either through the discharge (at a rate that goes as the speed) or back to suction, by leakage driven by the pressure difference (\(c_L\)) or by a loss that does not grow with it (\(c_s\)). Compressor data, not heat-pump data¶

The coefficients are fitted to standalone compressor performance and then frozen. Heat-pump catalogue COP is never used to fit them — a catalogue COP mixes compressor, conductance, fan and frost effects, and fitting the compressor to it would let a conductance error hide inside an efficiency coefficient. The catalogue set is what the assembled model is checked against afterwards (Validation). No number of a heat-pump catalogue is read anywhere in the fitting pipeline.

That principle was widened in one place, and only there.

The low-speed shape of the rotary types could not be decided by compressor data. Two sources carry it and they disagree: at 30 rev/s the three machines of Shao et al. (manufacturer polynomials of 2004, power basis not stated) have 0.72, 0.77 and 0.85 of their efficiency at 60 rev/s, the machine Ossorio-Santiago & Navarro-Peris measured in 2019 has 0.98. There is no third rotary source with a speed sweep. Both readings are in the library as rotary_speed_variant of tmhp.compressor_efficiency.CompressorSpec:

  • shao_informed fits all three rotary sources in one shared shape. At the 2019 test condition it gives 0.905 at 30 rev/s.

  • ossorio_anchored gives every speed record of the 2004 machines its own level, so they inform only the pressure-ratio shape and the speed terms rest on the 2019 measurement. It gives 0.968.

The default, shao_informed, was chosen by a rule written down before the measurement it uses: run the EN 14825 part-load trajectory of the next subsection for both variants and count the cases whose ratio COP(D) / COP(A) lies inside the 10th to 90th percentile of the certified Heat Pump Keymark population; a difference of three cases or more decides. The archive’s README records the result: 30 of 30 cases for shao_informed against 24 of 30 for ossorio_anchored, and a median distance from the certified median ratio of 0.041 against 0.115 in logarithm. The present library returns the same counts.

What this costs

The shape of the EN 14825 trajectory was used to choose the speed shape of the rotary types. For those types — the default among them — the trajectory-shape check is not independent validation: that the default passes it (Does the coefficient set survive both tests?) confirms the choice and nothing more. It is a choice between two shapes that were each fitted to compressor data alone; no coefficient was fitted to heat-pump data. The level of the trajectory, the catalogue parity set (Validation) and the A−7/W55 gate (High lift: hot water in cold weather) were not used and remain independent.

The same comparison holds evidence that points the other way. The README records it and so does this page:

  • The intermediate points agree better with the variant that was not chosen. Over the low-temperature cases the median COP(B) / COP(A) and COP(C) / COP(A) are 1.42 and 1.82 for shao_informed and 1.54 and 2.07 for ossorio_anchored; the ratios of the certified medians are 1.52 and 2.06. (A weaker comparison than the one the rule uses: only percentiles per point are published, and a ratio of medians is not the median of a ratio.)

  • The level check is passed by 9 of 30 cases with shao_informed and by 16 with ossorio_anchored.

  • Point D lies outside the rotary data under either variant, in speed (the data start at 30 rev/s and the machine runs at 15) and in pressure ratio (about 1.6; the data start at 1.9). Both values of COP(D) / COP(A) are extrapolations, and the choice rests on how each form extrapolates.

  • One independent check does not separate the variants: the two R290 machines of a manufacturer whose specification states the power at the inverter input imply an inverter efficiency of 0.90–0.91 under shao_informed and 0.89–0.90 under ossorio_anchored.

  • The part-load curve at held temperatures, measured after the choice. With frost left out, the default 9 kW R32 air-to-water unit loses 16.7 % of its full-load COP by the time it reaches its speed floor at +7 °C air and 35 °C leaving water, 27.0 % at +7 / 45 °C and 34.0 % at −7 / 45 °C under shao_informed; under ossorio_anchored it loses 3.9, 12.7 and 14.5 %, and its curve peaks at 56 to 74 % of the nameplate instead of 78 to 96 %. With R290 the falls are 5.0, 16.4 and 22.9 % against 1.5, 11.4 and 10.6 %. Published part-load curves of inverter air-to-water units peak near half of the maximum frequency, or rise all the way to the lowest speed. In return ossorio_anchored matches the air-to-air catalogues less well (COP MAPE 9.37 % against 8.52 %).

The default was left as the rule chose it for this release (owner decision of 2026-11-16, taken with these numbers), and the choice between the two shapes is to be reopened with the co-author’s review. Until then a study of part-load or seasonal performance should be run with both variants: where they differ, the difference is the uncertainty of the rotary shape.

Limits and provisional items¶

The rotary types are provisional. The default is one of them. Their shape rests on five machines: three manufacturer polynomials of 2004 for R22 whose power basis is not stated, one R290 machine measured across speed at a single condition, and one fixed-speed machine measured up to a pressure ratio of 6.3. There are no rotary data above a pressure ratio of 6.8. The inverter twin rotaries the default is named after contribute one rating point each, so they set its level and nothing of its shape.

The level is the manufacturers’ level, and independent measurements sit lower. Carried to the catalogues’ rating condition (R410A, 7.2 / 54.4 °C, 60 rev/s) with the scroll form, the level of the Copeland polynomials is an \(\eta_{oi}\) of 0.767 and that of the BITZER calculation data 0.728; the default, the median of the residential-size machines, is 0.765. The measured scrolls are at 0.639, 0.663 and 0.758 (the three AREP machines) and 0.648 (Cuevas & Lebrun), the published functions of Guth & Atakan at 0.720: between 1 % and 16 % below the default, three of the four measured machines by 13 to 16 %. For the rotary types the manufacturers’ rating points give medians of 0.705 (R32), 0.733 (R410A) and 0.744 (R290) for inverter twins and 0.655 (R410A) for fixed-speed singles; the one measured rotary, a fixed-speed machine, is at 0.630 (R32) and 0.643 (R410A) — about 2 % below the catalogue level of its own class and 11 to 12 % below the inverter twins. Every one of these measured machines is fixed-speed and line-fed, or a laboratory machine of 2009; a modern inverter compressor may well be better than they are, and nothing in the archive decides by how much, because it holds no independent measurement of the efficiency level of one. If the measured machines are the better guide, the default efficiency is optimistic by up to that margin.

The power basis of the rating points is assumed. The catalogues of the inverter twin rotaries do not print whether the rated power includes the inverter; it is read as excluding it. One check bears on it: two R290 machines of another manufacturer (GMCC), whose specification does state the inverter-input power, are matched by the default model only with an inverter efficiency of 0.90–0.91, where the drive model has 0.94 at that speed. For those two machines the model’s electrical input is 4 to 5 % low. The scroll level comes mostly from Copeland powers measured at the input of a drive that is not named; they were divided by the median drive model, so that level carries the spread between inverters, about 1.5 % in efficiency.

Refrigerants without rating points take the pooled level. That covers every refrigerant but R32, R410A and R290 for the default type — R134a and R1234yf in particular — and it is an extrapolation in suction pressure. The three levels that do exist span a factor of 1.15 (an \(\eta_{oi}\) of 0.705 to 0.744 at the rating condition). The fixed-speed type has one check: six R290 single rotaries, marked as under development in their catalogue, sit 4 % below what the pooled level gives.

The split of the loss is weakly identified: four measured scroll machines, whose own values of \(f\) run from 0.67 to 1.00, and for the rotary types four points of one machine. It moves the discharge temperature and the heating duty at a given electrical input, not the overall efficiency.

Below 30 rev/s the default is an extrapolation, and a steep one at a high lift. The default speed floor is 15 rev/s; the rotary data cover 30 to 120 rev/s. The form is followed as it is (efficiency_extrapolated is set), and with shao_informed it falls quickly: for a machine rated at 40 rev/s running R32, \(\eta_{oi}\) drops by 13 % between the rated speed and 15 rev/s at a pressure ratio of 2.2 and by 26 % at 5.4, while the drive efficiency goes from 0.923 to 0.832. In a machine, with frost left out, the default 9 kW R32 air-to-water unit at −7 °C outdoor air and 45 °C leaving water has a COP of 2.75 at full load, 2.76 at 75 % load and 1.81 at its speed floor (16 % of the nameplate); at +7 °C and 35 °C it has 4.46, 4.53 and 3.72 (24 %). Whether real inverter rotaries lose that much is exactly what the two speed sources disagree on. On the EN 14825 trajectory the default sits below the certified 10th percentile at a part-load point in 18 of the 30 cases and the variant with the shallower fall in 2; that variant is instead above the 90th percentile at point D in 12 (next subsection).

The volumetric efficiency of the rotary types above a pressure ratio of 6.8 is an extrapolation. Its fall with the pressure ratio comes mostly from the 2004 polynomials; the measured machine falls more slowly. For R32 at the rated speed the selected fit gives 0.736 at a pressure ratio of 8 and 0.684 at 10; the slope of the measured machine alone gives 0.763 and 0.719, that of the 2004 machine alone 0.728 and 0.673. That range is the stated uncertainty. It bears on the capacity a machine reaches in cold weather.

The rotary volumetric efficiency passes one at a low lift and a high speed. Its level \(c_0\) is 1.13, so for R32 the form exceeds one above 1.25 times the rated speed at a pressure ratio of 1.5 and above 1.74 times at 2.0 (it gives 1.03 and 1.01 at twice the rated speed). The value is then set to one, by the rule of the Summary.

The non-default variant can exceed one. ossorio_anchored has a term in \(PR^{(k-1)/k} - 1\), which turns negative when the effective exponent falls below one: with a wet suction, and at a high pressure ratio with a saturated one as well. At a pressure ratio of 4.9 with R290 and a suction quality of 0.7 the form gives \(\eta_{oi}\) = 1.04. Over a grid of five refrigerants (suction saturated at −25 to 7 °C, quality 0.7 to 1.0, pressure ratio 3 to 10, 30 to 120 rev/s for a machine rated at 60) it is above one in 292 of 3,500 states: from a pressure ratio of 6 at a quality of 0.9 (up to 1.24 at a ratio of 10) and up to 1.87 at a quality of 0.7. The isentropic efficiency is then set to one. The default variant has no such term: it gives 0.71 at that state, and its largest value on the grid is 0.76.

The scroll type has not been checked on a machine it describes. No unit of the validation set has a scroll without injection: the four air-to-water units with a scroll have vapour or flash injection, which the single-stage cycle does not represent, and the six others have rotaries. The five adopted air-to-air units have swing compressors and are run as the twin-rotary type. The scroll form is the better-founded of the three as a compressor model, and the least tested inside a heat pump.

Sources disagree on the scroll’s shape at a high pressure ratio. Above a ratio of 7 the Copeland polynomials fall faster than the model and the BITZER data and the R404A AREP machine more slowly. The built-in volume ratio and the relieved share are not stable when the Copeland machines are left out (\(r_v\) moves by 0.31, \(z_v\) goes to zero).

What is deliberately absent¶

  • No fit to heat-pump catalogue COP, and no per-machine tuning: a default machine is its type, its refrigerant and its rated speed.

  • No automatic choice of type. Every model is a twin rotary unless you say otherwise, whatever its capacity. Larger machines are often scrolls; compressor_type="scroll" has to be asked for.

  • No injection. Vapour- and flash-injection compressors are excluded from the fit and are not modelled.

  • No refrigerant-specific shape. The refrigerant enters through the fluid properties and, for the rotary types, through the level.

  • No bound outside the data. The forms are followed as they are; the earlier validity-domain guard and the lower limit on the isentropic efficiency are gone with the map they protected. efficiency_extrapolated tells you when a result lies outside the data hull of the type.

Where the earlier maps went¶

The quadratic map shipped from October to November 2026 (BITZER-three-C3-absolute-N-2026-10-06), the correlations before it (v2026-09-24) and the older sets are no longer in the library, and no alias is left for them. They are records: validation/coefficients/recorded_maps/ evaluates each of them number for number, for the comparison in the next subsection and for tests whose scenarios were recorded with them, and validation/coefficients/README.md lists the folders. The quadratic map can be refitted from its tracked raw points with the reproduce.py of its folder, which reproduces the released coefficients to within 3e-13 in predicted efficiency.

Does the coefficient set survive both tests?¶

A compressor description fitted on compressor measurements still has to work inside a heat pump. Whether it does is a question with two halves:

  • Shape. EN 14825 lowers the required duty and the flow temperature together across four test points, and Heat Pump Keymark publishes what real machines declare at each of them. A coefficient set can be checked against the certified population (Part-load behaviour).

  • Level. A set can sit on the certified median and still miss every individual machine. That is what the catalogue parity set answers (Validation).

Five compressor descriptions were run through both, identically, with the current model code. One of them is the previous release, on the same tree: it is the “before” of the change of compressor model.

Parity figures are the ten adopted air-to-water units; the held Fujitsu catalogues are excluded from every headline. The defaults row is the air-to-water row of Validation: six of the ten units are run as twin rotaries and four as scrolls, where the four other rows put one description on all ten. The rows are not scored on the same points. A point the model cannot deliver at the catalogue’s duty is not scored, and which points those are depends on the description: of the 153 points of the ten units, 136 are evaluated with the defaults and 132 with the previous release (138, 145 and 140 for the first three rows). Every row carries the frost and defrost adjustment of the air-source boiler (Frost and defrost), which lowers each description’s COP by 16 to 21 % at and below +2 °C outdoor (median over those points); without it the five rows read 53.3 / 18.4 / 7.1 / 12.4 / 11.5 % with biases of +53.3 / +17.1 / +2.0 / +10.2 / +6.5 %.

Three things to read off it.

The compressor description carries most of the model, and the catalogues do not separate the default from the map it replaced. An ideal compressor is 35.7 % out. The shipped models come to 8.34 % with a −5.02 % bias; the previous release, on the same tree, to 8.30 % with −2.10 %. The error is the same and the bias is 2.9 points more negative. The speed-independent constant set comes to 7.7 % and the pre-refit legacy-v1 set to 11.3 %, and the order of all four depends on the frost adjustment as much as on the compressor: without it they read 12.4 (previous release), 11.5 (defaults), 18.4 and 7.1 %. The case for the present model is the compressor-level evidence of the previous subsections — how it behaves beyond its data, the drive loss, the type. This table neither adds to it nor takes from it.

The shape passes — which, for the default, was the criterion of a choice. Across fifteen combinations of capacity, refrigerant and sizing ratio the modelled COP rises at every step A → D in all fifteen runs of each application (96.9 % of the 9,062 certified low-temperature records and 98.7 % of the 9,044 medium-temperature ones do the same), and the A-to-D gradient lands inside the certified p10–p90 in all thirty runs: median 2.80 modelled against 2.63 certified for the low-temperature application and 2.77 against 2.86 for the medium-temperature one. Frost lowers point A and so steepens both; without it the two medians are 2.44 and 2.46. The gradient criterion is not a sharp one: the previous release passes it in all thirty runs as well (medians 2.34 and 3.00), and so does the constant set, which has no speed dependence at all; ideal passes in 20 and legacy-v1 in 15.

The level is too low at part load. Nine of the thirty runs have all four points inside the certified p10–p90: six of the fifteen low-temperature runs (R32 three of nine, R410A all three) and three of the medium-temperature ones (the R290 runs). No run is outside at point A. The others are below the 10th percentile at the part-load points — at C (+7 °C, 35 % of the design load) in 18 runs, 6 low- and 12 medium-temperature, and at B (+2 °C, 54 %) in 14 of those, 2 and 12 — except the three low-temperature R290 runs, which are above the 90th percentile at point D. Read as percentiles of the certified population, the R32 9 kW low-temperature trajectory (design load two thirds of the nameplate) sits at the 45 / 12 / 10 / 63rd percentile at points A / B / C / D, with C just under the 10th (a COP of 5.36 against 5.37), and the medium-temperature one at 41 / 3 / 1 / 34.

The points that fail are the ones the compressor runs slowly at. At A it turns at 46 to 81 rev/s; at B at 21 to 32, below the 30 rev/s where the rotary data start in 27 of the 30 runs; at C at 15 to 17 and at D at 15, the speed floor. That is the range in which the default speed shape is an extrapolation and in which the two speed sources disagree (Limits and provisional items), and the variant that was not chosen passes this criterion in 16 runs. The previous release, on the same tree, passes it in 18 (5 low- and 13 medium-temperature) and fails on the other side: above the 90th percentile at point A in ten low-temperature runs and at point B in two medium-temperature ones. Against it the default’s COP is 12 % lower at point A of the low-temperature application and 17 and 22 % lower at points B and C of the medium-temperature one (medians over the runs).

The count has moved three times in November 2026, and the committed verdicts give each step: 25 of the 30 runs met the criterion in the results of 14 November, 14 once the default inputs came from catalogues (displacement and airflow; 15 November), and 9 now, with the semi-empirical model, the approach-temperature conductances and the plate-condenser sink. The previous map meets it in 18 runs on the present tree, so of the last step the tree around the compressor accounts for 14 → 18 and the compressor model for 18 → 9. Which input moved which point within those steps was not separated. Nothing was adjusted to improve this count.

Certified COP is measured with defrost, and what the model does about frost decides this criterion as much as the compressor does. Without any adjustment the same R32 trajectories sit at 92 / 29 / 10 / 63 and 86 / 5 / 1 / 34 — above the 90th percentile at point A in 12 of the 15 low-temperature runs, no run passing. With the EnergyPlus adjustment applied in full at every load they sit at 43 / 1 / 10 / 63 and 38 / 0 / 1 / 34 — point B below the 10th percentile in all 30 runs, no run passing: that adjustment is a function of outdoor temperature and humidity alone and corrects a unit at half load like one flat out. With the load on the coil taken into account (frost at part load; no constant added) point A keeps over nine tenths of the correction and point B a quarter of it.

Point C of the medium-temperature trajectory

An earlier edition of this page marked point C of the medium-temperature application as an artefact: the operating-point search starved the outdoor coil below the compressor speed floor. That search now scores candidates by electrical input per unit of heat delivered (tmhp._opt_utils). That the default is low at this point today is not that artefact: with the previous map the same search puts the R32 9 kW machine at the 77th percentile there. See Part-load behaviour.

Provenance¶

The source documents are archived locally but not committed: publisher PDFs and supplementary datasets are not redistributable. The tracked half is validation/registry/sources.yaml, which records what each document is, where to obtain it and the SHA-256 of the copy the scripts were run against. A reader who obtains the same document from the manufacturer and gets the same checksum is running on identical bytes, and everything under validation/data/ follows from those bytes by the scripts in validation/extraction/.
