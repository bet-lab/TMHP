Where the defaults come from¶

TMHP decides a lot on your behalf. Hand it a capacity and a refrigerant and it produces heat-exchanger conductances, a compressor displacement, a rated air flow and the efficiency model of a compressor. That convenience is the point of the library — but it is only worth having if each of those numbers can be traced to a published document rather than to somebody’s judgement, and if you can rerun the step that produced it.

This page is that trace. Every row links to the source and to the script that reads it.

The one rule behind all of it

Published literature reports the conductance, displacement or efficiency of particular machines. Nobody publishes a rule expressed per unit of capacity — which is exactly what a model that sizes its own components needs. So the numbers below are not taken from a paper. They are derived from populations: manufacturer catalogues that rate an entire product range at one declared condition, which makes models of different capacity comparable by construction. The citations are for the method, the rating standard and the correlation; the numbers are measured across many machines.

A consequence worth stating plainly: validating against a catalogue is never calibrating against it. No parameter below is fitted to any unit in the validation set. The heat-pump catalogue that entered the choice of the compressor maps’ axes did not separate the candidates, so the choice was made on the measured compressor (The comparison that chose the axes).

Heat-exchanger conductance¶

The problem¶

Every model generates its heat-exchanger conductances from a single declared capacity, so what has to be established is a capacity-normalised rule for each exchanger — not any particular machine’s conductance. Real machines do not publish a conductance either. What their service manuals, installation manuals and test reports print at a rated point is the refrigerant pressure at a service or compressor port, next to the entering air or water temperature, the flow and the capacity. That is evidence at the level of the heat exchanger, and it is what the cycle needs: the conductance is only the means by which the model arrives at the saturation temperature the compressor works against.

What the conductance means¶

Every refrigerant-side exchanger in TMHP is an isothermal refrigerant against a single-phase fluid, ε = 1 − exp(−UA/C) and Q = ε C |T_in − T_sat|. There is no refrigerant pressure drop and no desuperheating or subcooling zone, so the model’s saturation temperature has to be the one at the compressor’s ports for the pressure ratio to be right. A default conductance is therefore defined as the effective conductance that reproduces, at the rated point, the saturation temperature at the pressure the machine prints. Line and coil pressure drop and the single-phase zones are inside it; it is not the U·A one would compute from the coil geometry.

Which approach temperature transfers¶

For one operating point, the inlet temperature difference ITD = |T_in − T_sat|, the leaving-end approach ITD − Q/C, the LMTD and UA/Q convert into each other exactly. The choice only matters when a value measured on one machine is carried to another of a different capacity and flow: what is held fixed? That is a question the data can answer. Per exchanger and refrigerant role, each candidate law is fitted with one product line held out (product lines that share hardware count as one) and scored by how well it predicts the saturation temperature of the held-out units at their own flow and duty. The rule, fixed before the data were collected: the simplest law within 10 % of the lowest error ships, if the stratum has at least three product lines and two manufacturers.

The leaving-end approach wins or ties in every stratum a model uses, and a fixed inlet difference — the obvious convention — is the worst everywhere. The reason shows in the data: manufacturers build several capacities on one chassis, and on a shared coil the inlet difference grows with the load while the leaving approach stays put. Holding it fixed gives the conductance in closed form,

\[UA = C \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{\dot Q}{C\,\Delta T_{\mathrm{out}}}\right)\]

with C the fluid’s heat capacity rate at the reference flow and Q̇ the exchanger’s own duty. A solution exists for every positive duty and flow. A fixed inlet difference has none once Q̇ ≥ C · ITD — a reference flow 15 % below the one assumed can be enough — and conductance per unit capacity ignores the flow altogether.

The duty is the exchanger’s, not the nameplate: as a condenser the outdoor coil of a split unit rejects 1.24 W per watt of cooling nameplate (the nameplate plus the compressor work), and as an evaporator absorbs 0.76 W per watt of heating nameplate, both archive medians (tmhp.default_inputs.hx_duty_per_nameplate()). The reference flow is the rated airflow of the coil (Maximum fan airflow), the rated loop flow, or the rated circulation flow of a plate condenser.

The 59 sources are manufacturer service and installation manuals (Mitsubishi Electric, ClimateMaster, WaterFurnace, Carrier, Bosch, Toshiba, Gree), the AHRI low-GWP AREP system test reports, an ORNL mini-split test and a KTH air-to-water prototype; the archive’s README lists them with their hosts, the conversions and the anomalies the documents contain. Outdoor coils are pooled across product classes: split and air-to-water outdoor coils are one construction and their leaving approaches agree (as condensers 2.5 K for wall-mounted splits, 2.3 K for ducted splits and 3.7 K for air-to-water units). Indoor coils are not pooled — 6.8 K for wall-mounted units, 2.6 K for ducted ones, 2.8 K for water-to-air units — and each model takes the stratum of its own kind of indoor unit, where one ships.

The same outdoor coil has a smaller effective conductance as an evaporator than as a condenser (a median ratio of 0.72 over the units printed in both modes), so each role takes the law of its own stratum: AirSourceHeatPump has UA_ou_rated for cooling and UA_ou_evap_rated for heating, and GroundSourceHeatPump UA_ground_rated and UA_ground_evap_rated. A conductance you pass for the first and not the second serves both roles; a value you give is never split.

These values were not chosen by catalogue COP: the laws and their constants come from the archive and the hold-out rule alone, and the catalogue comparison of Validation was run only after they were fixed. With them the comparison still under-predicts, so the conductance is not where the remaining bias sits.

Every default conductance is recorded in model.default_input_estimates["UA_…"] with its law, the spread of the population, the hold-out error, the sources and whether the duty lies outside the archive’s range (extrapolated). A conductance you pass is used as given and has no entry there.

For the outdoor coil as a condenser the new evidence lands almost exactly on the previous rule, which was derived from different data by a different route (The previous rule). The larger changes are elsewhere: an outdoor coil as an evaporator now has about four fifths of its condensing value; the indoor coil of the ground-source model has 1.6 to 1.9 times the conductance; and the boilers’ sink is a different structure, so its UA_tank_hx is not comparable with the earlier one by itself — what the cycle sees is K_tank_hx (next section), which is below the earlier Q/5. Small machines get more outdoor conductance per watt than large ones because their rated airflow per watt is larger (the airflow laws have an intercept).

How wide the band is, and what that costs you

The leaving approach of a stratum is a median. Between the 10th and 90th percentile of the product lines it spans 0.9–3.9 K for outdoor coils as condensers, 2.0–4.5 K as evaporators, 0.7–3.3 K for plate condensers, 1.0–6.6 K and 1.6–6.5 K for coaxial source exchangers as evaporators and condensers, and 1.5–6.6 K for water-to-air indoor coils. That width is real hardware, not measurement noise: at the same duty and flow, two machines at either end of the outdoor condensing band condense about 3 K apart. Even the best law misses the saturation temperature of a held-out product line by 1.2 to 2.9 K (RMSE).

So a single vendor-neutral default describes a typical machine, and will under-predict a high-efficiency product line and over-predict a lower-efficiency one. If you know the machine, pass its conductances (UA_ou_rated, UA_ou_evap_rated, UA_iu_rated, UA_tank_hx …). Validation backs the conductance out of each validation unit’s own rating point, and decomposes the rest of the residual, which is not all conductance.

Note

Quote the band on the same basis as the rule. The archive’s spread is a spread of leaving approach; its spread in UA per watt also carries the spread of airflow per watt, and is wider.

Where this does not apply

  • Wall-mounted indoor coils. No value is shipped. Every row that gives duty, airflow and pressure at one point comes from one manufacturer (Mitsubishi Electric, six product lines), and the evidence disagrees on the quantity the law uses: 6.8 K of leaving approach on the declared duty and airflow, about 3.6 K on the discharge-air temperature the same tables print, about 3.3 K from a second manufacturer’s pipe temperatures at an assumed rated load. The inlet difference agrees at 22 K. So AirSourceHeatPump keeps UA_iu_rated = 0.16 × hp_capacity, no longer tied to the outdoor coil, and records it as provisional with that reason.

  • Frosted operation. Frosting rows are excluded; frost and defrost are handled by the defrost adjustment, not by the conductance.

  • Microchannel coils. The archive has none; the strata are fin-and-tube coils.

  • Wet cooling coils. Indoor coils run wet in cooling and TMHP’s coils are dry, so those rows are excluded. The indoor values come from the dry condensing role and are used in both modes.

  • Immersed or wrapped tank coils. No measured row pairs a condensing pressure with a tank temperature, so no value is issued. Build the boiler with m_dot_w_rated=0 and pass UA_tank_hx.

  • Water-to-water machines. The archive’s water-to-water rows come from one manufacturer whose heating tables print suction pressures below their own low-pressure cut-out; the liquid-source boilers borrow the water-to-air coaxial evaporator.

The boilers’ sink¶

The tank heat exchanger of AirSourceHeatPumpBoiler, GroundSourceHeatPumpBoiler and WaterSourceHeatPumpBoiler is an external plate condenser fed with tank water by a circulation pump, the structure of monobloc air-to-water units. T_tank_w is the water entering the condenser. The rated circulation flow is m_dot_w_rated = hp_capacity / (c_w · dT_w_rated) with dT_w_rated 5 K (the EN 14511 30/35 °C rating), and the heat the exchanger carries per kelvin of condensing temperature above the entering water is K_tank_hx = C_w (1 − exp(−UA_tank_hx / C_w)). AirSourceHeatPumpBoiler takes a per-call m_dot_w; the liquid-source boilers run at m_dot_w_rated. m_dot_w_rated=0 selects the earlier form without circulating water, UA_tank_hx working against the tank temperature directly; with UA_tank_hx = hp_capacity / 5 it is the earlier machine.

A rating condition names the leaving water (W35, W55). The catalogue comparisons and operating_range() hand a boiler the water EN 14511 enters with — the leaving water less the standard’s rise, 5 K up to 45 °C, 8 K at 55 °C, 10 K at 65 °C — and, for AirSourceHeatPumpBoiler, the flow that carries the duty at that rise (tmhp.standard_conditions.en14511_water_rise(), tmhp.standard_conditions.en14511_condenser_water()). This replaces the earlier convention of a tank 2.5 K below the leaving water with no flow.

The previous rule¶

Until November 2026 the outdoor coil of AirSourceHeatPump had UA_ou = hp_capacity / 5 in both roles and its indoor coil 0.8 × UA_ou; the boilers had UA_tank_hx = hp_capacity / 5 without circulating water, the air-source boiler’s outdoor coil 0.7 × UA_tank_hx, and the ground-source models 0.18 × hp_capacity on the source side and 0.08 × hp_capacity for the indoor coil. hp_capacity / 5 came from 1,414 component coils in catalogues rated under EN 328 (352 evaporators, median UA/Q 0.190) and ENV 327 (1,062 condensers, 0.146), converted to the nameplate basis with 1 + 1/EER and corroborated by twelve Trane packaged units’ published coil geometry through the Wang, Chi & Chang (2000) air-side correlation. The scripts under validation/extraction/ (ua_transfer_law, en328_inversion, trane_geometry, dt_convention) still reproduce it.

It was replaced for two reasons. The rating standards fix the temperature difference at which a component coil is rated, so the populations say how much conductance a coil has at that difference, not which approach a heat pump runs at; and residential mini-splits, the product class of the air-to-air validation units, were outside that evidence. For outdoor coils the new evidence lands on almost the same value. An earlier version of that work also reported a pooled regression of UA/Q against air flow (slope −0.020, R² 0.001) as evidence that the two are unrelated; inside one rating standard they are linked by an identity, so the regression meant nothing and was withdrawn, leaving the overlap of the two standards’ bands and the geometric route as the evidence.

Compressor displacement¶

The machine has to move enough refrigerant to carry the nameplate duty at the nameplate condition:

\[V_{\mathrm{disp}} = \frac{\dot Q_{\mathrm{nom}}} {\Delta h \; \rho_{1} \; \eta_{\mathrm{vol}} \; n_{\mathrm{rated}}}\]

Both \(\Delta h\) and \(\rho_1\) come from CoolProp at a declared rating point, so the dependence on the working fluid is pure fluid property rather than a tabulated coefficient. That dependence is not small: per kilowatt of nameplate the displacement runs from about 3.0 cm³/rev for R32 to 7.3 for R134a.

What is left, \(\eta_{\mathrm{vol}}\, n\), belongs to the compressor, and compressor catalogues print it. Each row gives a displacement and a capacity at a declared rating point, so \(\eta_{\mathrm{vol}}\, n = \dot Q / (V \rho_1 \Delta h)\) row by row. Across 41 rows of inverter twin-rotary compressors from two manufacturers (LG and Mitsubishi Electric SCI; 14 families, R32 and R410A, 2.9 to 14.9 kW) it is 53.2 rev/s — 0.887 of the 60 rev/s the rows are published at. Predicting a family, a manufacturer or a refrigerant from the others misses the printed displacement by 2.7, 2.6 and 3.8 %.

The default displacement is that relation at the catalogues’ own rating point (evaporating 7.2 °C, condensing 54.4 °C, suction 18.3 °C, liquid 46.1 °C), with the nameplate capacity standing in for the compressor’s catalogue capacity (tmhp.default_inputs.estimate_displacement()). Every model — AirSourceHeatPump, GroundSourceHeatPump, AirSourceHeatPumpBoiler, GroundSourceHeatPumpBoiler and WaterSourceHeatPumpBoiler — uses it when V_cmp_ref is left out, and says so: model.default_input_estimates["V_cmp_ref"] holds the value, the data release, the capacity range of the catalogue rows, whether the capacity lies outside that range (extrapolated) and the source documents. A V_cmp_ref you pass is used as given and has no entry there.

One limit remains, and it belongs to the rule. A ground-source boiler is still rated high — at 8 kW, 63.1 rev/s with R32, 65.5 with R410A, 66.7 with R290 and 71.8 with R134a, above the 60 rev/s the catalogue rows are published at — because its nameplate condition, B0/W55, is harsher than the catalogue rating point (7.2 / 54.4 °C) at which the rule reads the nameplate: a compressor sized to deliver the nameplate there has to turn faster to deliver it from brine at 0 °C. Its speed ceiling, 120 rev/s, lies above the 110 rev/s the efficiency data reach, as that of every default machine does. If you know the compressor of such a machine, pass V_cmp_ref and rps_max.

The class rating points¶

tmhp.compressor_speed.refrigerant_aware_displacement() evaluates the same relation at any declared rating point and rated speed. Two are declared per equipment class — EN 14511 A7/W35 for air-to-water, ISO 5151 T1 for air-to-air — and their rated speed is the one quantity that carries real uncertainty, since the result is exactly inversely proportional to it. They are not what the default uses; they are the record of how the rated speed of each class was bounded before compressor catalogues were collected.

For air-to-water it is not a guess. Inverting the relation against the nine Panasonic Aquarea units whose compressor displacement is published by the manufacturer’s compressor division gives a median rated speed of 42 rev/s, and

Tip

A displacement error is second order for steady-state COP: the speed solver absorbs it into speed, and the result moves only through the speed dependence of the efficiencies. Where it matters is the modulation envelope — displacement and rps_min jointly fix the lowest duty the machine can deliver. If you know your machine’s displacement, pass V_cmp_ref.

Maximum fan airflow¶

A fan default has to say which airflow it is. Catalogues print several fan steps, and the one the model needs is the largest: the rated airflow is where the fan stops (dV_*_fan_a_max defaults to it), where the coil conductance is stated and where the fan power is anchored. So the default is the largest fan step a product publishes, fitted against the capacity of the same duty — cooling airflow against cooling capacity, heating against heating — with one law per product class. Families are weighted equally, and of a proportional, a linear and a power law the simplest within 2 % of the best family-hold-out error is kept (tmhp.default_inputs.estimate_max_airflow(); Q is the capacity in kW, the result m³/s).

Where the catalogue airflow meets the dry-coil model

The indoor laws give 150 to 160 m³/h per kW for wall-mounted units of 9 kW and above and for floor-standing units of any size. TMHP’s coils are dry, so the whole nameplate is sensible heat, and at that airflow the room air has to change by about 20 K: the refrigerant then stands 20 to 25 K from the room air at nameplate capacity (a 15 kW unit condenses at 42 °C in a 20 °C room and evaporates at 4.6 °C in a 27 °C one).

That is why the approach-temperature search box of AirSourceHeatPump (dT_approach_bounds) is 1 to 30 K — the box the catalogue parity runs have always used. With the earlier upper bound of 20 K the box itself was the limit at the rating point: a default unit reached its nameplate at 35/27 °C only up to 8 kW (98 % at 9 kW, 89 % at 15 kW; a floor-standing unit 96 % at any capacity), and in heating at −7 °C outdoors fell short from 6 kW (86 % at 8 kW). At 30 K every default unit from 1.5 to 50 kW, of either indoor class and either rated duty, delivers its nameplate at both rating points. In mild weather the ceiling of the default 4 kW unit is then 189 % of the nameplate at 7/20 °C, where a coil is still at the edge of the search, and 166 % at 35/27 °C, where the compressor sets it (126 % and 124 % at 20 K).

The air-to-water outdoor stratum is the weakest of the airflow laws and says so. It has 59 rows from 13 families and 6 manufacturers (Daikin, LG, Mitsubishi Electric, Samsung, Toshiba, Vaillant), and the manufacturers differ: predicting each from the other five misses by 22.7 % on average, between −27 % and +25 % by manufacturer, and a family held out by 20.2 % — both a little over the 20 % the other strata were held to. It ships as the default by the owner’s decision (2026-11-15), as a central value with that spread stated: a default is chosen by the evidence for the input, and against the nine Panasonic units of the validation set — which are not in the stratum — the law misses the published airflow by 14.1 % (8.0 % low on average) where the constant missed it by 32.5 %. model.default_input_estimates["dV_fan_a_rated"] carries the sources, the hold-out errors and the spread between manufacturers (a unit’s own airflow can differ from the default by about ±23 %); a published airflow passed as dV_fan_a_rated replaces it.

Compressor efficiency¶

The default compressor efficiencies are three maps of one compressor, version ossorio-r290-te-2026-11-21 (tmhp.compressor_efficiency): a 46 cm³/rev R290 scroll, measured at 134 operating points. The same maps serve every refrigerant and every compressor; the library has no compressor type to choose (#217). A particular machine is followed by calibrating the maps to its data (#90), not by a form of its own. This section says what the maps are, what they were fitted to, how their axes were chosen and where they are weak. The authority for every statement is the archive, validation/coefficients/ossorio-r290-te-2026-11-21/, and its README.

Two things to know before the detail

  • The maps describe one compressor, an R290 scroll. Every default machine is given its efficiencies, whatever the refrigerant and the compressor of the machine (Limits).

  • Uncalibrated, the defaults run low against the catalogues: a COP bias of −5.9 % for the air-to-water units and −12.1 % for the air-to-air units (Validation).

What is modelled, and where each loss sits¶

The maps are fitted on the power the compressor and its inverter draw together, \(W\). Three quantities are computed from each measured point with CoolProp (propane):

\[\eta_v = \frac{\dot m}{\rho_1\, V N}, \qquad \eta_{ov} = \frac{\dot m\,(h_{2s} - h_1)}{W}, \qquad f_{loss} = 1 - \frac{\dot m\,(h_2 - h_1)}{W}\]

with \(h_2\) from the measured discharge temperature. \(\eta_v\) is the delivered fraction of the swept volume, \(\eta_{ov}\) the overall efficiency, and \(f_{loss}\) the share of the electrical input that never reaches the gas: the losses of the motor and the inverter and the heat the shell gives off. A model reports three quantities, as it always has: eta_cmp_vol is \(\eta_v\), eta_cmp is \(1 - f_{loss}\) and eta_cmp_isen is \(\eta_{ov} / (1 - f_{loss})\), so eta_cmp_isen · eta_cmp is \(\eta_{ov}\). eta_cmp_isen sets the discharge enthalpy, and so the heating duty and the discharge temperature; eta_cmp sets the electrical input E_cmp = ṁ (h_2 − h_1) / eta_cmp, which is therefore the power at the inverter input. There is no separate drive model (#217): the inverter loss is the one measured with this compressor, inside \(f_{loss}\). An efficiency you pass yourself (eta_cmp_isen=…) is a number or a function of the pressure ratio and the speed and is used as given.

The maps¶

With \(l = \ln PR\), \(n = N / 70\) (\(N\) in rev/s) and \(t = T_e / 10\) (\(T_e\) the evaporating saturation temperature in °C):

\[\eta_v,\ \eta_{ov} = c \cdot [1,\ l,\ l^2,\ l^3,\ n,\ n^2,\ l\,n,\ t] \qquad f_{loss} = c \cdot [1,\ l,\ l^2,\ l^3,\ n,\ n^2,\ l\,n]\]

Each map is fitted to the 134 points by least squares. The speed is absolute: \(n = 1\) is 70 rev/s whatever the rated speed of the machine, and no quantity is read relative to the rated speed. The coefficients are in the archive’s coefficients.json, and the constants of the library module are emitted from it.

The data¶

ossorio_thesis_r290_134.csv holds 134 measured points of a 46 cm³/rev R290 scroll compressor at 30, 50, 70, 90 and 110 rev/s with a superheat of about 10 K, evaporating at −30.2 to 25.1 °C. They are extracted row by row from R. Ossorio’s PhD thesis (Universitat Politècnica de València, Appendix 1, PDF pp. 244–252); the page and the row of each point are in the file. They are the tests of Compressor A of Ossorio & Navarro-Peris (2023). The thesis tabulates the power of the compressor and that of compressor and inverter together; the maps use the second.

The efficiencies computed from the points (points.csv) agree with the author’s tabulated values to 0.005 for \(\eta_v\) and 0.008 for \(\eta_{ov}\). The correlations of Ossorio, Marchante-Avellaneda & Navarro-Peris (2024, Appl. Therm. Eng. 244:122666) were fitted to the same tests (133 points); the PR–N quadratic of 2026-11-01 (#90) was fitted to those correlations, not to the measured points. Every input of the fit is committed.

Why the evaporating temperature¶

The measured efficiencies depend on \(T_e\). At one pressure ratio and speed the measured \(\eta_{ov}\) and \(\eta_v\) differ with the evaporating temperature, and without the \(T_e\) term the residual follows it (figures/te_residuals.png of the archive). With the term the cross-validated RMSE falls from 0.034 to 0.022 for \(\eta_{ov}\) and from 0.015 to 0.008 for \(\eta_v\).

The correlation behind the earlier quadratic says the same. In the 2024 correlation the specific consumption carries \((P_c + 6.70)/(P_e + 0.51)\), which is not the pressure ratio; at a pressure ratio of 3 and 70 rev/s its \(\eta_{ov}\) rises from 0.60 to 0.70 between \(T_e\) = −10 and 15 °C. The map gives 0.62 and 0.69 there.

\(f_{loss}\) does not need the term: one changes its error by 0.0001.

The comparison that chose the axes¶

Four candidates were compared (#218; comparison/ of the archive): the PR–N quadratic of 2026-11-01; (a) the PR–N form above without \(T_e\); (b) the same form with \(T_e\), the one adopted; and (c) a PR–N map on the terminal basis with the inverter of Ossorio & Navarro-Peris (2023), Eq. 5. Each was scored against the 134 points and calibrated to the heating catalogue of the Fujitsu ASYG30LMTA (50 rows) with the protocol of #90: no calibration, then the level \(\lambda_{ov}\), then \(\lambda_{ov}\) and a shift of the peak (\(\alpha_r\), \(\alpha_N\)). The calibration is not in the library; it was run on the branch feat/compressor-catalogue-fitting at 9f349df1, and the scripts are in #218.

Limits¶

One R290 scroll for every machine. The maps are one compressor’s, and they are applied unchanged to every refrigerant, every compressor and every capacity: to the rotary and swing compressors of most of the validation set and to R32, R410A and R134a as much as to R290. The refrigerant enters through the fluid properties, not through the efficiency. Nothing in the archive says how far another compressor sits from this one. Injection compressors are not modelled.

Uncalibrated, the defaults run low. Applied unchanged, the shipped defaults give a COP bias of −5.9 % for the ten air-to-water units and −12.1 % for the five air-to-air units, cooling and heating alike (Validation). How much of it is the compressor was not separated.

Over-compression at a low pressure ratio. A scroll has a built-in volume ratio and over-compresses below it, so the maps fall steeply at a low pressure ratio: at 70 rev/s and \(T_e\) = 10 °C, \(\eta_{ov}\) is 0.46 at a pressure ratio of 1.5 against 0.69 at 4. A rolling-piston rotary has no built-in volume ratio (an inference about other machines: the archive holds no rotary data). It bears on low-lift operation: cooling in mild weather, and low flow temperatures in mild weather.

Outside 30–110 rev/s the maps are extrapolated. The default speed floor is 15 rev/s and the ceiling 120 rev/s or more, so part load below 30 rev/s and full load in cold weather above 110 rev/s lie outside the data. The speed terms, a quadratic in \(n\), are followed as they are, and efficiency_extrapolated is set. At a pressure ratio of 3 and \(T_e\) = 0 °C the map’s \(\eta_{ov}\) is 0.649 at 70 rev/s, 0.616 at 30 and 0.582 at 15 rev/s, and 0.598 at 110 and 0.572 at 120 rev/s. In a machine, with frost left out, the default 9 kW R32 air-to-water unit at −7 °C outdoor air and 45 °C leaving water has a COP of 2.64 at full load, 2.68 at 75 % load and 2.59 at its speed floor (21 % of the nameplate); at +7 °C and 35 °C it has 4.33, 4.47 and 4.63 (30 %).

The volumetric map passes one at a low pressure ratio and a warm evaporator (1.009 at a pressure ratio of 1.5, \(T_e\) = 20 °C and 70 rev/s). The value is then set to one, by the rule of the Summary.

Where the earlier maps went¶

The type-specific semi-empirical models of 2026-11-15 (semi-empirical-2026-11-15: a scroll and two rotary types chosen with compressor_type, two rotary speed shapes and a separate drive model) are no longer in the library (#217). Their archive and their pipeline, validation/coefficients/semi-empirical-2026-11-15/ and validation/compressor_maps/semi_empirical/, remain as records. The quadratic map of October 2026 (BITZER-three-C3-absolute-N-2026-10-06), the correlations before it (v2026-09-24) and the older sets are records too: validation/coefficients/recorded_maps/ evaluates them number for number for tests whose scenarios were recorded with them, and validation/coefficients/README.md lists the folders. BITZER data are no longer used; the test and validation records that still hold them are to be removed (#222).

Does the map survive both tests?¶

A compressor description fitted on compressor measurements still has to work inside a heat pump. Whether it does is a question with two halves:

  • Shape. EN 14825 lowers the required duty and the flow temperature together across four test points, and Heat Pump Keymark publishes what real machines declare at each of them. A description can be checked against the certified population (Part-load behaviour).

  • Level. A description can sit on the certified median and still miss every individual machine. That is what the catalogue parity set answers (Validation).

Four compressor descriptions were run through both, identically, with the current model code. Neither half was used to fit the maps or to choose their axes.

Three things to read off it.

The compressor description carries most of the model. An ideal compressor is 35.7 % out. The shipped maps come to 7.94 % with a −5.86 % bias. The speed-independent constant set comes to 7.7 % and the pre-refit legacy-v1 set to 11.3 %, and the order of the three depends on the frost adjustment as much as on the compressor: without it they read 18.4, 7.1 and 9.6 % (defaults). The case for the present maps is the compressor-level evidence of the previous subsections; this table neither adds to it nor takes from it.

The shape passes. Across fifteen combinations of capacity, refrigerant and sizing ratio the modelled COP rises at every step A → D in all fifteen runs of each application (96.9 % of the 9,062 certified low-temperature records and 98.7 % of the 9,044 medium-temperature ones do the same), and the A-to-D gradient lands inside the certified p10–p90 in all thirty runs: median 2.46 modelled against 2.63 certified for the low-temperature application and 2.92 against 2.86 for the medium-temperature one. Frost lowers point A and so steepens both; without it the two medians are 2.13 and 2.53. The gradient criterion is not a sharp one: the constant set, which has no speed dependence at all, passes it in all thirty runs as well; ideal passes in 20 and legacy-v1 in 15.

The level passes in every run. All thirty runs have all four points inside the certified p10–p90; legacy-v1 has 20 such runs, constant and ideal none. Read as percentiles of the certified population, the R32 9 kW low-temperature trajectory (design load two thirds of the nameplate) sits at the 34 / 21 / 21 / 22nd percentile at points A / B / C / D, and the medium-temperature one at 36 / 44 / 52 / 43.

The part-load points are the ones the compressor runs slowly at. At A it turns at 40 to 62 rev/s; at B at 17 to 26, below the 30 rev/s where the data of the maps start, in all 30 runs; at C and D at 15, the speed floor. The level at B, C and D therefore rests on the maps followed below their data (Limits).

The count has moved four times in November 2026, and the committed verdicts give each step: 25 of the 30 runs met the criterion in the results of 14 November, 14 once the default inputs came from catalogues (displacement and airflow; 15 November), 9 with the semi-empirical models, the approach-temperature conductances and the plate-condenser sink, and 30 now, with the maps of one R290 scroll. Only the compressor model changed in the last step. Which input moved which point within the earlier steps was not separated. Nothing was adjusted to improve this count.

Certified COP is measured with defrost, and what the model does about frost decides this criterion as much as the compressor does. Without any adjustment the same R32 trajectories sit at 90 / 54 / 21 / 22 and 86 / 74 / 52 / 43, and three of the 15 low-temperature runs are above the 90th percentile at point A; the other 27 runs pass. With the EnergyPlus adjustment applied in full at every load they sit at 30 / 1 / 21 / 22 and 33 / 1 / 52 / 43 — point B below the 10th percentile in all 30 runs, no run passing: that adjustment is a function of outdoor temperature and humidity alone and corrects a unit at half load like one flat out. With the load on the coil taken into account (frost at part load; no constant added) point A keeps over nine tenths of the correction and point B a quarter of it.

Point C of the medium-temperature trajectory

An earlier edition of this page marked point C of the medium-temperature application as an artefact: the operating-point search starved the outdoor coil below the compressor speed floor. That search now scores candidates by electrical input per unit of heat delivered (tmhp._opt_utils). See Part-load behaviour.

Provenance¶

The source documents are archived locally but not committed: publisher PDFs and supplementary datasets are not redistributable. The tracked half is validation/registry/sources.yaml, which records what each document is, where to obtain it and the SHA-256 of the copy the scripts were run against. A reader who obtains the same document from the manufacturer and gets the same checksum is running on identical bytes, and everything under validation/data/ follows from those bytes by the scripts in validation/extraction/.
